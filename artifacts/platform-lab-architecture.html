Platform lab · AWS

What builds what, and what calls what

A lab of five machines running Elasticsearch, two Kubernetes clusters, an API gateway and an identity provider. Every arrow here was executed and verified, not sketched from intent.

The mental modelTwo planes, and everything belongs to one

Almost every confusing question about this system resolves once you know which plane a thing lives in. Terraform and Ansible build; Kong and Rancher serve. They never run at the same time, and they fail in completely different ways.

Build plane

runs once, then exits

Terraform makes machines and disks exist. Ansible installs and configures software on them. Helm deploys apps into Kubernetes. When these finish, nothing of them is left running.

Runtime plane

stays up, answers requests

Elasticsearch, Kong, Keycloak, Rancher, RDS. These are what a user or another service actually talks to. They know nothing about Terraform or Ansible.

If you are new to thisFour things that surprise everyone

A host cannot reach its own public IP

A check running on es-01 that points at es-01's public address goes out to the internet gateway and back into a firewall that only admits your laptop. It times out while the service is perfectly healthy. Use 127.0.0.1 or the private address for anything a host does to itself.

Managed services have no host to log into

RDS has no SSH and no public address, so there is nothing for Ansible to connect to. It is configured through es-01, which is allowed to reach it. That is the general shape for every managed service: you configure a host that is permitted to talk to it, not the thing itself.

On EKS, "too small" can mean IP addresses

A node with 65% of its CPU free refused to schedule anything, reporting Too many pods. On EKS every pod gets a real address from the VPC, handed out by network interfaces attached to the instance — and the number of those is fixed by instance type. A small instance allows 17 pods no matter how idle it is.

So there are three separate ways to be "full", not two: memory, CPU, and addresses. The third is invisible on Google Kubernetes Engine and on k3s, which give pods addresses on a private overlay network instead. Same manifests, same sizes, one cluster fits them and the other does not.

Two kinds of "too small" look identical

One node froze because it ran out of memory; another failed because a burstable instance had spent its CPU credits and was getting a fifth of the CPU it appeared to have. Both look like "it's slow and things are crashing".

free -m tells you the first. top and the st (steal) column tell you the second — and steal cannot be fixed from inside the machine at all.
